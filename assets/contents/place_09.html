A full refrigerator is not a meal

You have thousands of English words in storage. You recognise them instantly when you read them. And yet, at 10:04 on a Tuesday, in front of a client, the shelf looks empty.

That is the difference between passive vocabulary — words you recognise — and active vocabulary — words that arrive when you open your mouth. Learners spend years adding ingredients to the fridge and almost no time cooking.

The test

A word is active when you can produce it in your own sentence, at speed, without translating. Anything else is still an ingredient.

The personal dictionary — four steps per word

  1. Capture it. Every new word you hear or read that day goes into one place. One notebook, one file, one note app.
  2. Label it. Is it a noun, a V1, a V2, an adjective? This is what tells you where it can sit in a sentence.
  3. Own it. Write two or three sentences of your own — about your work, your product, your week. Not the dictionary's sentence. Yours.
  4. Pair it. Learn the words that travel with it. English is full of fixed pairs: launch a campaign, close a deal, hit a target, raise a concern. Collocation tools such as OZDIC and the Cambridge dictionary's audio are built for this.

Ten words a week beats a hundred

Ten words taken all the way to active — labelled, used in your own sentences, paired with their partners — will change how you sound in a meeting. A hundred recognised words will not.
